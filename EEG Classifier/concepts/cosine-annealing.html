Concept · Optimization

Cosine Annealing

Sprint, then slow into the finish line — a smooth learning-rate schedule.

TL;DR

The learning rate controls how big a step the optimizer takes each update. Too big and you overshoot; too small and you never get there. Cosine annealing smoothly decays the learning rate from a high starting value down to near zero following the shape of a cosine curve — big steps early to explore, small steps late to settle precisely into a minimum.

The problem it solves

Training a neural network is a walk down a hilly loss surface. The learning rate is the length of each step.

A high learning rate is great at the start: the model is far from any good solution, and you want to cover ground quickly. Bold steps let you escape flat plateaus and don't get stuck in shallow ditches.

A high learning rate near the end is terrible: the model is close to a good minimum, and bold steps will launch you right past it. You need small, precise adjustments to settle in.

A fixed learning rate is either too big late or too small early. A schedule — something that starts high and decreases over training — gives you both phases.

How cosine annealing works

The formula: 

lr(t) = lr_min + 0.5 × (lr_max - lr_min) × (1 + cos(π × t / T_max))

Where t is the current epoch and T_max is the total number of epochs. Plotted, it looks like the top-left quadrant of a cosine wave:

The derivative of the cosine is zero at both endpoints — the curve leaves the starting value gently and approaches the end value gently. That smoothness is the whole pitch of cosine annealing versus step or exponential decay, which drop the rate in abrupt jumps or along a sharper exponential curve.

In your project

From 04_eegnet_sweep.ipynb and eeg_online_classifier.py:480+: 

opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=8e-4)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(
    opt, T_max=epochs, eta_min=1e-5
)

for epoch in range(epochs):
    for xb, yb in dataloader:
        ...
        opt.step()
    sched.step()    # advance the schedule one epoch

Your learning rate smoothly decays from 8e-4 (the Adam default is 1e-3; you chose slightly more conservative) to 1e-5 (~80× smaller) over the course of training.

Why these particular numbers

ChoiceWhy
lr_max = 8e-4 Slightly below Adam's default of 1e-3. With a small dataset and batch size 16, a marginally smaller starting rate reduces the chance of early instability.
lr_min = 1e-5 Not zero — you want some learning even at the end, enough to keep making tiny corrections. Far smaller than the starting rate, so the end phase is genuinely in "polish" mode.
T_max = epochs Schedule reaches its minimum exactly when training ends. No leftover schedule, no chopped-off schedule.

What else it could look like

ScheduleShapeWhen to prefer
Step decay Drops by factor at fixed epochs Classic ResNet/ImageNet recipe. Simple but abrupt.
Exponential Continuous exponential decay Faster early decay than cosine; can undershoot.
Cosine annealing Smooth cosine curve The modern default. No tuning required beyond start/end rates.
Warm restarts (SGDR) Cosine curve that resets mid-training Periodically re-explores the loss surface. Good for escaping sharp minima.
OneCycle Triangle: warmup up then decay down Fast-convergence recipes; strong on small datasets if tuned well.

Pitfalls

Calling sched.step() in the wrong place. PyTorch's scheduler advances one step per call. If you put it inside the batch loop instead of the epoch loop, your schedule compresses into a fraction of training and the learning rate floors out hundreds of epochs early.

Scheduler running after the optimizer. Best practice: opt.step() before sched.step(). In some older PyTorch versions, getting the order wrong gave a warning — in current versions it's silent. Easy to miss.

Over-reliance on the schedule. A good schedule won't save a bad starting learning rate. If the initial lr is wildly wrong, cosine annealing just decays a bad number towards zero.

Interview-ready answer

I use cosine annealing on the learning rate, decaying from 8e-4 down to 1e-5 over the full training length. Early in training the model is far from a good solution, so larger steps help it explore. Late in training it's close to a minimum and smaller steps are needed to settle without overshooting. Cosine has smoother derivatives than step decay, so there are no abrupt transitions where training can destabilize.

Going deeper (if they push)

  • Warm restarts (SGDR): restart the cosine cycle periodically. Each restart kicks the model out of its current local minimum; the cycle that follows settles it into (hopefully) a better one.
  • Linear warmup: start with a very low learning rate for the first few epochs and ramp up to lr_max before starting cosine decay. Critical for large-batch training; less important for batch size 16.
  • OneCycle: warmup + cosine decay in a single triangle. Super-convergence papers use it to train CIFAR in 1 minute.
  • Loss-based schedules (ReduceLROnPlateau): drop the LR only when validation loss stops improving. Responsive but can make experiments non-reproducible across runs.
