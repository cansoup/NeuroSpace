Concept · Regularization

Dropout

Benching random players so no one on the team becomes indispensable.

TL;DR

During training, dropout randomly sets a fraction of neurons to zero on every forward pass. This forces the network to spread learning across many units instead of letting one or two become single points of failure. At inference time all neurons are used — dropout is disabled.

The problem it solves

Neural networks are massively over-parameterized. With enough capacity and small datasets, they memorize instead of generalize. A specific neuron might learn to fire only for trial #47's particular noise pattern. Training accuracy climbs. Test accuracy stalls. Classic overfitting.

Worse: neurons co-adapt. Neuron A learns "I fire when Neuron B fires, and together we recognize this specific quirk." The network becomes a fragile chain of inside jokes that only works on training data.

How it works (the soccer-team analogy)

Imagine a soccer team that always practices with the same 11 starters. Each player learns to rely on the specific tendencies of the others. If one gets injured mid-match, the system collapses.

Now imagine the coach picks a random subset to sit out every practice. Each player must learn to play alongside many different partners. Nobody can over-specialize. On game day, with everyone on the field, you have a team of flexible generalists instead of a brittle chain of co-dependents.

That's dropout. On each training step, each neuron has probability p of being temporarily zeroed. The rest of the network has to solve the problem without it. Next step, a different random subset is dropped.

The math (one line)

During training, each activation is multiplied by a Bernoulli mask: 

y = x * mask      mask ~ Bernoulli(1 - p)

At inference:
y = x             (no mask — all neurons active)

Modern implementations (including PyTorch's nn.Dropout) use inverted dropout: during training, kept activations are scaled by 1/(1-p) so the expected output magnitude matches inference. This is why you don't have to do anything special when you switch to model.eval().

Why 0.6 is high (and why you use it anyway)

Typical dropout values across the field:

ValueWhere you'd see it
0.1 – 0.2Large CNNs trained on millions of images
0.3 – 0.5The default for fully-connected layers in medium datasets
0.5 – 0.7Small datasets where overfitting is the dominant concern

Your project tuned dropout over {0.5, 0.6, 0.7} and landed on 0.6. That's aggressive. It's also the right call for a 90-trial dataset: with so few samples, the cost of under-regularizing (memorizing specific trials) is much higher than the cost of slightly under-fitting.

The fact that the sweep preferred 0.6 over 0.5 or 0.7 tells you something useful: the model genuinely wants aggressive regularization. If the best value had been 0.3, you'd have evidence that more data or a smaller architecture was the real answer.

In your project

Dropout is applied after each pooling layer in both EEGNet blocks (eeg_online_classifier.py:174, 187): 

self.block1 = nn.Sequential(
    ...
    nn.AvgPool2d((1, 4)),
    nn.Dropout(dropout),    # 60% of activations zeroed per step
)

self.block2 = nn.Sequential(
    ...
    nn.AvgPool2d((1, 8)),
    nn.Dropout(dropout),    # applied again after block 2
)

Two dropout layers for a 3-block network is standard EEGNet practice. The same rate is used in both — there's no evidence that tuning them separately would help on data this small.

Pitfalls

Too high: If you set dropout to 0.9, the network only sees 10% of its own activations on each pass. It can't learn. Training loss plateaus. Symptom: train and validation loss both stay high.

Forgetting model.eval(): at inference, you must switch the model to eval mode — otherwise dropout is still active and predictions are randomly noisy. load_model() calls this before returning the model, so your online classifier is safe.

Dropout + BatchNorm ordering: there's a long-running debate about BN → Dropout vs Dropout → BN. EEGNet uses the first order (BN is inside each block, dropout is after the pool). You don't need to pick a side — just don't reorder them mid-experiment.

Interview-ready answer

Dropout is a regularization technique that randomly zeros a fraction of activations during training — in my case, 60%. It prevents neurons from co-adapting and forces the network to learn redundant, robust representations instead of brittle ones. I use an aggressive rate because the dataset is small — about 90 trials — so overfitting is my dominant failure mode. The value was chosen by the hyperparameter sweep, not picked by hand. At inference, PyTorch disables dropout automatically when the model is in eval mode.

Going deeper (if they push)

  • DropConnect: zeros weights instead of activations. Similar idea, used in some older vision models.
  • Spatial dropout (nn.Dropout2d): zeros entire feature maps instead of individual activations. Makes more sense for convolutional layers where adjacent pixels are already correlated — you wouldn't see much from dropping single pixels. EEGNet uses plain nn.Dropout; spatial dropout is an optimization you could try.
  • Stochastic depth: a block-level dropout used in ResNets (randomly skip entire residual blocks during training).
  • Dropout ≈ Bayesian approximation: Gal & Ghahramani (2016) showed that keeping dropout active at inference and averaging many forward passes approximates sampling from a Bayesian posterior. This is "Monte Carlo dropout" and gives you uncertainty estimates for free — potentially useful for a BCI where "I'm not sure" is a valid output.
