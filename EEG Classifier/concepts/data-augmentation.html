Concept · Training data

Data Augmentation

Showing the model the world from angles it hasn't seen — without collecting new data.

TL;DR

Data augmentation creates plausible variations of your training examples on the fly: slight noise, small shifts, gentle distortions. The model sees a "new" sample every epoch even though your dataset hasn't grown. It's the single cheapest way to improve generalization on small datasets — when your augmentations reflect real-world variation the model will actually encounter.

The problem it solves

Deep networks generalize to the extent that training data covers the true distribution. With 90 EEG trials, your training set covers a pin-prick of that distribution. The model memorizes those specific 90 recordings and then encounters a new session where the electrode impedance is a little different, or the participant shifted a bit, or the room has slightly different interference — and performance falls off a cliff.

Augmentation lets you cover more of the distribution without collecting more data. You apply random, realistic perturbations to each training sample. The model learns to be invariant to those perturbations, which means it handles the real-world version of them too.

How it works

During training, every batch fetch runs the raw sample through a pipeline of random transforms before feeding it to the model: 

for epoch in range(n_epochs):
    for X_batch, y_batch in loader:
        X_batch = augment(X_batch)       # ← different random perturbation every time
        loss = criterion(model(X_batch), y_batch)
        loss.backward()
        ...

The label doesn't change — augmentations are designed to preserve the class identity. A left-hand motor-imagery trial is still a left-hand motor-imagery trial after you've added a little noise to it. If a "left" trial became a "right" trial under your augmentation, you're doing it wrong.

The augmentations in your project

From augment() at eeg_online_classifier.py:112-144:

1. Gaussian noise (σ = 0.08)

Adds a small random value to each sample, independently. Simulates sensor noise, thermal fluctuations in amplifier electronics, and minor movement artifacts that add broadband noise. 

X += np.random.randn(*X.shape).astype(np.float32) * 0.08

2. Random time shift (±40 samples)

Slides the entire 2.5 s window by up to 160 ms in either direction. Simulates the fact that motor-imagery onset has a fuzzy boundary — the participant didn't start imagining at the exact millisecond the trial was marked. The model should not key on the exact time alignment of the trial trigger.

3. Per-channel amplitude jitter (×U[0.8, 1.2])

Multiplies each channel independently by a random scalar between 0.8 and 1.2. Simulates electrode impedance drift and session-to-session amplifier gain differences — the biggest source of cross-session failure in real BCI deployments.

4. Channel dropout (30% chance of zeroing one channel)

Sets one random channel's entire signal to zero with probability 0.3. Simulates a detached or broken electrode. Forces the model to not collapse when one sensor fails — essential for a 4-channel headset where losing one electrode means losing 25% of your data at inference.

Why each of these is the right choice for EEG

Good augmentations reflect real-world variation the model will actually see at inference. Bad augmentations inject noise the model shouldn't have to handle, which hurts training without making deployment easier.

AugmentationReal-world thing it mimics
Gaussian noiseAmplifier thermal noise, environmental EMI
Time shiftImprecise trial onset timing
Amplitude jitterElectrode impedance / gel drying / session drift
Channel dropoutBroken or detached electrode

Things you should not augment for motor-imagery EEG:

  • Channel permutation: electrode positions are meaningful (C3 vs C4 is the whole point). Swapping them would swap class labels.
  • Frequency shifting: mu (8-12 Hz) and beta (13-30 Hz) bands are the signal. Shifting them up or down destroys the label.
  • Time reversal: motor imagery has forward temporal dynamics.

Why it's disabled in the tuning notebook

The notebook version of training turns augmentation off. That's intentional: the comparison there is against classical baselines (CSP, FBCSP, Riemannian methods), which don't typically use augmentation. To compare architectures fairly, you hold the training recipe constant.

Once the winning architecture is chosen, augmentation gets turned back on for the production retrain. The extra 3-8% accuracy from augmentation is a separate, additive gain layered on top of the architecture choice.

Pitfalls

Too strong. If you add noise at σ = 1.0 to already-normalized data, you've drowned the signal. The model can't learn because the augmented samples look nothing like what you'll see at inference. Start conservative and increase only if overfitting persists.

Label-breaking. Channel permutation on spatially-meaningful data, frequency-band shifts on band-specific signals, reflection on causally-directed data — these are all label-destroying transforms. If you added one by mistake, your training loss would plateau at chance.

Applied at inference. Augmentations are training-only. Apply them at inference and you're adding noise to every prediction. If augment() is ever called inside run_online(), something has gone wrong.

Not applied consistently to the validation fold. Tricky one: during CV, you augment the training fold but not the test fold. Applying augmentation to test data is test-time augmentation (TTA), a different technique — don't conflate them.

Interview-ready answer

Because the dataset is small, I use four EEG-specific augmentations during training: low-amplitude Gaussian noise, small random time shifts, per-channel amplitude jitter, and stochastic single-channel dropout. Each one simulates a real-world variation a deployed BCI actually encounters — sensor noise, imprecise trial onsets, impedance drift, and electrode failures — so the model learns to be invariant to them. Augmentations are on during the production retrain and off during the hyperparameter sweep so architectures compare fairly against classical baselines.

Going deeper (if they push)

  • Mixup: linearly interpolate two samples and their labels. A strong regularizer in image classification; applied to EEG with mixed results.
  • CutMix: splice a chunk of one sample into another. Again, more common in vision.
  • SpecAugment: zero out random time bands and frequency bands. Standard for audio and adaptable to EEG spectrograms.
  • Generative augmentation: use a GAN or VAE to synthesize new samples. Powerful but easy to get wrong — generated samples need to be genuinely class-preserving, and validating that on a BCI is hard.
