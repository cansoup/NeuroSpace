Cross-Validation

How to grade a model honestly when your dataset is small.

TL;DR

Splitting your data once into train and test is like grading a student on a single question — they might get lucky. Cross-validation grades them on several questions in rotation, so the final score is a trustworthy average instead of a coin flip.

The problem it solves

You have 90 EEG trials. You decide to train on 72 and test on 18. You get 80% accuracy. Is the real answer 80%?

Maybe. Or maybe those 18 test trials happened to be easy ones — clean recordings from a cooperative moment. If you'd held out a different 18, you might have gotten 62%. With a small dataset, a single train/test split is one roll of the dice.

Cross-validation rolls the dice many times and averages the result. The mean tells you how good the model is. The standard deviation tells you how stable that estimate is. Both matter.

How it works

K-fold cross-validation is the standard recipe:

  1. Chop your data into K equal-sized pieces (folds). With 5 folds and 90 trials, each fold has 18 trials.
  2. Train on K−1 folds. Test on the remaining one. Record the score.
  3. Rotate: a different fold becomes the test set. Retrain from scratch. Record again.
  4. Repeat K times. Every trial eventually serves as test data exactly once.
  5. Report the mean and standard deviation of the K scores.

Why "stratified" matters

If you shuffle 90 trials (30 left, 30 right, 30 both) and chop them into 5 folds, random luck might put 15 "left" trials into one fold and only 2 into another. That fold now evaluates on a class the model barely saw.

Stratified folding preserves the class ratio inside every fold. If the dataset is ~33% each class, every fold is ~33% each class. Same question, fairer grading.

In your project

From notebooks/utils/eval.py: 

skf = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
for train_idx, test_idx in skf.split(X, y):
    X_tr, X_te = X[train_idx], X[test_idx]
    y_tr, y_te = y[train_idx], y[test_idx]
    y_pred = fit_predict(X_tr, y_tr, X_te, y_te)
    fold_scores.append(f1_score(y_te, y_pred, average='macro'))

Five folds on 90 trials is a deliberate compromise:

  • Too few folds (e.g. 2): each fold leaves out half your data. Models can't learn much from 45 trials.
  • Too many folds (e.g. 30): each test set is tiny (3 trials), so fold scores are noisy and the standard deviation becomes meaningless.
  • 5 folds lands in the middle: 72 training trials per fold, 18 test trials per fold.

The random_state=42 isn't magic — it's just a fixed seed so re-running gives the same fold assignments. Reproducibility matters when you're comparing hyperparameter configs; otherwise each run shuffles differently and you can't tell signal from noise.

Pitfalls to know about

Data leakage: if you fit anything (scaler, PCA, even mean-centering) on the full dataset before splitting, the test fold has peeked at its own answer key. Preprocessing must happen inside each fold, not before.

CV-overfitting: if you try 10,000 hyperparameter configurations, one will win by luck alone. Your 12-point grid is small enough to keep this risk low — adding dimensions to the sweep (e.g. also searching LR and batch size) trades genuine tuning for CV gaming.

Subject leakage (EEG-specific): if you had data from multiple people and shuffled trials across subjects into folds, you'd be testing on trials from subjects you trained on — inflating the score. You only have one subject, so this doesn't bite here, but it's the reason BCI papers use Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) CV.

Interview-ready answer

I used 5-fold stratified cross-validation with a fixed random seed. Each fold preserves the class proportions of the original dataset. I report mean ± standard deviation of macro-F1 across the five folds — the standard deviation tells me how stable the estimate is, not just the point accuracy. I use the same CV split for every hyperparameter configuration so results are directly comparable, and I refit all preprocessing inside each fold to avoid leaking test data.

Going deeper (if they push)

  • Leave-One-Out (LOO): K = dataset size. Every trial is its own test fold. Most data for training, but brutal variance and K times slower.
  • Nested CV: outer loop for honest performance estimation, inner loop for hyperparameter selection. The right way to tune without inflating the reported score.
  • Time-series CV: for data with temporal order, you can't shuffle — you'd train on the future and test on the past. Use expanding-window or rolling-window splits instead.
